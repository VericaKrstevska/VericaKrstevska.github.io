North Macedonia's issue with waste management:

Spreading awareness through paper recycling

Тhe issue behind the motivation

According to the State Statistical Office of North Macedonia 99.8% of the municipal waste ends up in landfills.

Landfills generate methane and carbon dioxide from the decomposing organic waste, leach toxins, heavy metals and other harmful substances into the soil and water in the surroundings, and are a threat to wildlife.

In the years 2020-2025, on average 84% of the landfill waste was classified as “mixed municipal waste” which can have a various composition and is therefore unknown. Additionally, the Ministry of Environment and Physical Planning estimates 2000 illegal landfills (while the real number is possibly higher), and more than 50 landfills which do not meet waste management standards. All of these, whether legal or not, are dealt with in the same way: by incineration. This is oftentimes the reason for wildfires in the summer months which not only pollute the air around settlements but also spread to the forests and threaten wildlife in the vicinity.

Moreover, due to a lack of enforcement of regulations surrounding environmental protection, industrial waste containing hazardous chemicals often ends up released in nature. Import of waste from other countries further worsens the waste (mis)management in the country, while a uniform and established waste selection and recycling infrastructure is virtually non-existent. Efforts are being made by non-governmental associations such as Pakomak – a non-profit company comprised of some of the leading food and beverage manufacturing companies in the country which collaborates with collection, storage and recycling companies, thus providing organization of packaging waste management to manufacturers, importers or even municipalities [1].

Paper recycling

11 551 tons of paper goes to landfills every year (avg. 2020-2025).

Paper is one of the easiest materials to recycle. It is made of cellulose fibers usually obtained from wood, crops, or wastepaper which go through pulping – a process that separates the cellulose from other components either mechanically by grinding or chemically – giving less yield but stronger fibers. The pulp is spread onto a wire mesh and pressed to remove water, after which it can go into the dryers – this is the most energy consuming step of the process, after which the paper can have different surface treatments depending on its purpose [2]. When recycling paper the process also includes de-inking which removes ink and other contaminants.

This process can relatively easily be recreated at home using some basic materials.

Materials and Methods

Paper: used notebooks, printed paper, old newspapers, packaging, etc.

Water softens and helps separate the cellulose fibers and makes the consistency easier to work with and shape, it also washes away residues and contaminants.

Pot/wash basin: for submerging the paper in water.

Blender: to make the pulp slurry.

Wire mesh (and frame): to dry the paper on.

Sponge: to speed up the removal of water and drying.

The paper is torn/cut to pieces and soaked in water overnight or up to a day. Then it’s blended until it becomes mushy, water can be added to get the wanted consistency. The pulp is then transferred onto a wire mesh which can be fixed to a frame to create paper with consistent size, or it can be spread on the wire and shaped according to one’s preferences. By gently pressing a sponge on the surface the water is soaked up, this additionally helps to make the paper thinner, smoother and with a more consistent thickness. The paper is then left to air-dry for at least two days depending on temperature and humidity.

Left: Adding soaked paper in the blender. Right: the wire mesh frame before pouring the pulp.

Results

The recycled paper obtained is sturdy, thicker than conventional paper, it has a rougher and more textured surface. It can be used for painting since the colors get absorbed well without tearing apart or getting damaged.

I experimented with adding flowers and leaves before drying to incorporate them in the paper and thus in the art piece.

Left: Daisies incorporated in recycled paper. Right: Ivy leaves in recycled paper.

Spreading awareness through art

For the next part of this project the goal was to spread awareness to the general public in an engaging and fun way: setting up a stand in a public place selling art done on recycled paper. To reach people I wanted to choose a busy spot with lots of visitors and close to nature, this was possible thanks to Complex “Lebedovo Ezero” who allowed me to set up my stand on their premises by the lake, surrounded by animals and the forest.

People were interested in the art, however they were especially intrigued by the paper: “ Why use recycled paper?”, “How did that idea come to be?”, “How was the recycling done?”, “Can anyone do it?”

These were some of the questions asked, and that was exactly my purpose. Through these questions I could share some facts about how waste is handled in the country, what can be done differently, and the effect of landfills on the environment and people’s health. I also invited them to question whether they really have to throw things away as opposed to reusing, repurposing, recycling or upcycling them, to be mindful of their consumption and choice in products, but also to be vigilant of their surroundings and proactive and vocal about the issues in their community.

Kids were frequent visitors of the stand initially intrigued by the paintings, but when prompted to observe and touch the paper or when asked if they know what recycling is, they quickly became curious to hear more. With them I shared some basic information about the importance of keeping nature clean while they were given blank recycled paper to draw on.
